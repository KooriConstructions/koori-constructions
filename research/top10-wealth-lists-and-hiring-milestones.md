### New name — Frank Lowy (Westfield)
Arrived in Australia in 1952 as a 21-year-old Holocaust survivor; met fellow immigrant John Saunders in 1953, opened a Blacktown delicatessen together. Deli/coffee-shop profits plus a bank loan funded their first land purchase — subdivided farmland that became Westfield Place, Blacktown (opened 1959): two department stores, a supermarket, 12 shops, 50 free car spaces — an explicitly American-style mall format, novel in Australia at the time. **The critical scaling decision:** rather than building an internal team first, they **floated Westfield Development Corporation on the ASX just 11 months after opening their first centre** (June 1960), raising capital from 300,000 shares at five shillings each — substituting public capital markets for internal hiring far earlier than anyone else in this research. Stepped back from executive leadership in the 2010s; Westfield sold to Unibail-Rodamco in 2018.

### New name — John Gandel (Gandel Group / Chadstone)
Took over the family's Sussan clothing chain in the 1950s, grew it to 200+ stores with brother-in-law Marc Besen. As retail margins compressed in the early 1980s, **reinvested retail cash flow into commercial property** rather than more retail — bought Chadstone Shopping Centre from Myer for $37M in 1983, founded Gandel Group the same year, sold his Sussan stake to Besen in 1985 to go all-in on property. Chadstone grew to 200,000+ sqm, the largest shopping centre in the Southern Hemisphere. Notable structural point: Gandel Group appears to route construction through external contractors rather than an in-house build arm — a pure investor/developer model, unlike Triguboff's build-and-hold-everything approach.

### New name — Perron family (Stan Perron)
Grew up poor in WA's goldfields; sold hand-carved wooden fretwork to miners at 14. Post-WWII: taxi business, milk bars, a loss-making ice rink, then **Perron Brothers earthmoving/trucking**, sold to Thiess in 1961 for his first real capital. Bought a WA Toyota distribution franchise, rode Toyota's boom, rolled the cash into shopping centres, industrial property and a large Wesfarmers stake. **The clearest, best-dated hiring milestone in this entire research effort:** Ross Robertson was hired as an accountant in **December 1981**, made **CFO in 1995** (14 years later), **CEO in 2011**, **Managing Director in 2018**. His own successor as CEO had himself served as CFO from 2019 — a deliberate internal-promotion culture, not stacked external senior hires.

### Pattern synthesis across the top 10
1. **Capital markets can substitute for early hiring** (Lowy went public within a year).
2. **An existing unrelated cash-generative business can fund the property pivot** (Gandel's retail chain; echoes Perron's Toyota franchise and Arnaout's pubs from Round 1).
3. **One trusted generalist hired early and promoted over decades beats a stacked executive team** (Perron/Robertson).
4. **A trucking/earthmoving/logistics base recurring before property** — Perron, Walker (already known), and Lang Walker's earthmoving origins all fit the same shape: **build an adjacent cash-generative operating business first, fund land from its surplus, rather than starting undercapitalised as a pure developer.**

### New name — Len Buckeridge (BGC Australia)
Trained as an **architect**, not a tradesman; founded his building business in Perth ~1959–60. Origin-defining move: a window-frame shortage during the WA building boom forced him to **build his own windowmaking factory** rather than wait on suppliers — the seed of "the Buckeridge model," vertically integrating brick, cement, roofing, plasterboard, insulation, asphalt and window manufacturing so BGC never depended on outside subcontractors for materials. Peak: $1.2B annual sales, 2,600 direct employees, 6,500 subcontractors, WA's largest home builder. Worked at his desk until the day he died, aged 77, in 2014. Family has since sold $1B+ of assets and exited new home-building.

### New name — Scott Hutchinson (Hutchinson Builders)
Fourth-generation, not a founder in the trade sense — the firm was founded in Brisbane in **1912** by an English immigrant carpenter. Scott entered full-time in **1986** as an "apprentice project manager," **deliberately placed under an experienced foreman** rather than handed authority — became Managing Director in 1992. Now Australia's largest privately owned construction company by revenue (**$3.35B FY25**). The lesson here isn't founding — it's the deliberate mentor-assignment even for a family successor.

### New name — Nigel Satterley (the closest structural match to Brad's goal)
Not a tradesman — WA's Levi's jeans distributor at 20, then a **$10,000 profit on his first land deal at 21** (advised in by banker Sir James McCusker). Reinvested that $10k to found **Statesman Homes**, grew it into WA's biggest home builder (650 homes/year), **then sold it in 1980** and pivoted to pure land development. Now Australia's largest independent private residential land developer (~$800–850M/year in project land sales, 180+ estates). **This sell-the-builder-then-become-a-developer sequence is the single most directly analogous data point to Brad's stated ambition found across both rounds of research.**

### New name — Rory Costelloe (Villawood Properties)
A **civil engineer**, not a tradesman. Villawood began in 1989 when a **syndicate of 16 friends/associates** (including a real estate agent, a lawyer, an accountant) each put in $10,000 to buy a 30-lot Bendigo block — capital pooling among white-collar professionals, not sweat equity. First metro-Melbourne expansion came 10 years later, in 1999. Now 21,000+ lots delivered, $250M turnover.

### New name — Judith Brinsmead (ADCO Constructions)
Former MinterEllison law partner who joined the family construction business in 1989 (17 years after its 1972 founding) and has chaired it for 30+ years — a professional/legal governance path rather than a trade-founder path.

### Pattern synthesis across the builder top 10
The honest finding: **dated hiring-milestone detail (first tradesperson, first foreman, first estimator, point of putting down the tools) is almost never publicly documented**, even for huge companies — this is a genuine gap in the public record, not a research failure. What did surface:
1. **Trade-background founders reinvested profit into capability, not headcount** — Buckeridge built a factory, Satterley bought land — "buy capability before you buy people."
2. **Professionally-trained founders (engineer Costelloe, architect Buckeridge, lawyer Brinsmead) skipped the tools stage entirely**, often pooling capital with other professionals rather than hiring tradespeople as employee #1.
3. **Dynastic firms show succession, not founding** — the formative "hire" is really a mentor-assignment (Hutchinson placed under a foreman in 1986) or a professional brought in from outside (Brinsmead from law).
4. **The clearest transition point in the whole builder list is a sale-and-pivot, not a hire** — Satterley sold his home-building company and re-founded as a land developer. If Brad wants one story to study line-by-line, it's this one.

## Part C — Hiring Milestones for the Founders Already Researched (Round 1 names)

**Research note:** page-fetch access was heavily restricted this round, so this relies more on search-result summaries than full transcripts — gaps below are flagged as "not retrievable," not "doesn't exist."

- **Graya (Rob & Andrew Gray):** Andrew brought institutional-grade QS/financial-services experience (ex-GRC Quantity Surveyors) in from founding — Graya never had a "no finance person" phase. Rob: *"it's really taken us 10 years of grinding to feel like we're really getting somewhere."* Now 125 staff; granular first-hire sequence not found.
- **McDonald Jones (Bill McDonald):** Founded 1987 with partner Phil Jones (exited 2000). OAM citation specifically for providing long-term jobs and building-industry cadetships — suggests a structured approach to workforce-building. Now 900 employees/2,500+ contractors.
- **ABN Group (Dale Alcock):** A materially different pattern — Alcock joined an *existing* multi-partner group (founded 1978) as the front-facing builder for a new brand, rather than building the finance/admin scaffolding himself from zero. Bought out Brown-Neaves' share in 2016. Resolve Finance (the group's mortgage-broking arm) is top-10 nationally — timing of when it was stood up wasn't found.
- **Thirdi Group:** Luke Berry and Bob Huxley already had a marketing/sales-minded partnership (an unrelated import business) *before* the property business existed. Luke Mahaffey joined 2013 as a consultant, 8 years after founding — unclear if earlier hires preceded him. Berry's current title (Director of Sales and Marketing) suggests sales stayed founder-led long-term.
- **Iris Capital (Sam Arnaout):** Founded 1995 as a hospitality/pub-and-casino operator — property development was added *onto* an established operating business, not the starting point. No hiring-sequence detail found.
- **Fortis/Pallas Group:** The clearest example of "finance expertise as a founding input, not an add-on" — three co-founders split by pre-existing discipline (law, structured debt, development) from day one in 2016. Grew to ~30 people managing $2B+ in ~5 years.
- **Mosaic (Brook Monahan):** Founded solo 2004; David Handley added as formal co-founder in **2012** — 8 years later — with a clean split (Monahan = vision, Handley = delivery). That gap itself is a data point about when Monahan needed a delivery partner, not just staff.
- **Kokoda (Mark Stevens):** Founded 1997 solo from a retail background (Jeans West), first project a house reno. No hiring-sequence detail found.
- **Aria (Tim Forrester):** A five-person founding group from day one in 2003 (Forrester, two school friends, his sister, one more). Co-founder Brent Liddell has called the first 6–7 years "the apprenticeship years."

### Overall synthesis (both rounds combined)
**The dominant, best-evidenced pattern across nearly every well-documented Australian builder/developer story is: pair with a finance/numbers person as co-founder before scaling headcount at all**, rather than the assumed sequence of "hire a bookkeeper once you can afford one." Graya, Fortis, Springfield, Aria, and arguably ABN Group (Alcock joining an existing multi-partner structure) all fit this shape. Sales/marketing, by contrast, tends to stay founder-owned far longer than finance does. **The practical implication for Brad: the single highest-leverage early move is likely finding or formalising a numbers-minded partner, not hiring staff in sequence.**

## Part D — Deep Content Sweep: What They've Actually Said

### Rob Gray (Graya) — richest content trail
- **Best single listen:** The Urban Developer × STAC Capital 3-part series, *"From Blueprint to Build: GRAYA's Growth Story"* — the only piece found, across every founder researched in either round, that explains **how a builder-developer actually structures project funding** (bank vs non-bank debt, presale leverage). theurbandeveloper.com/articles/from-blueprint-to-build-graya-s-growth-story-with-stac-capital
- *Everything Property* ep. 111 ("$1 Billion Dollar Property Developer Blueprint") and *Bothsides Podcast* ep. 147 — long-form, grind-focused, good for calibrating expectations.
- Quotes: *"Other than site acquisition, the design is a close second on what's most important on a project. If you nail it and get it right the buyers come to you, the funders come to you."* / *"There are spikes and hurdles everywhere in the building game and it's never smooth sailing."* / *"Having an in-house team rather than contractors provides us with greater control over the quality and availability."*

### Dale Alcock — thin personal content, one useful structural insight
No long-form podcast/interview located discussing his early funding or mistakes — appears to genuinely not exist publicly. One usable insight from a leadership-society write-up: growth "had been achieved without strategic planning; instead, organic growth and acquisitions, based on choosing the right person (acting as if they were the owner), matched to an opportunity" — **people-first delegation, not top-down planning.** ABN Group's ~20-business vertical+horizontal integration (building, land, finance, plumbing, concreting, roofing, cabinet-making) is a structural model Brad could copy in miniature via his existing trade relationships.

### Bill McDonald — press-only, pre-podcast generation
No podcast/YouTube content found. Quote: *"I've always believed in life you have to give to succeed."* Sold majority stake to Japanese trade buyer Asahi Kasei Homes in 2023 — his eventual scale-up capital came from a **foreign strategic buyer**, not private equity or IPO. As the most locally relevant Newcastle figure with essentially no public content trail, a direct approach to MJH Group (still Newcastle-based) may be more productive than searching for more.

### Sam Arnaout — zero long-form content found
Despite being an AFR Rich Lister and the most locally relevant name in this entire research effort, **no podcast or YouTube interview exists in searchable form.** Only Newcastle Herald project-announcement quotes: *"We could have built that anywhere, but we wanted to create something that had not been seen before in Newcastle."* If Brad wants Arnaout's actual playbook, it's a direct-approach situation, not a listen-and-learn one.

### Duayne Pearce — most prolific, most tactically useful for right now
Not a developer — a Brisbane builder turned business coach — but the most directly actionable content for Brad's *pre-development* problem. His **PAC Process** (**"Paid As Consultant"**) teaches builders to charge for pre-construction advice instead of quoting for free — a direct cashflow fix. Standalone course ~$1,350+GST (livelifebuild.com/pac-challenge/); paid ELEVATE membership includes 130+ business systems plus a 1:1 onboarding session (exact price unconfirmed — check the site directly).

### Justin Gehde — the relatable case study
His book, *Become a Million Dollar Property Developer*, is built entirely around his own first deal — **a 20-townhouse project that made well over $1 million** — the closest thing across all research to a step-by-step account of a small, realistic first project rather than a billion-dollar pipeline.

### Tim Willing — different family structure than assumed
Father Doug built homes since 1969; brother Dayne runs the construction side (Willing Homes); Tim spent 9 years as MD of Multiplex Living before founding Willing Property (development) in 2012 — a two-generations-of-builders-plus-corporate-son path, not a direct apprentice-to-developer one. Best source: *The Perth Property Show* ep. 272.

### Rob Flux (bonus — not previously covered, surfaced repeatedly as the field's biggest educator)
Runs Property Developer Network, Australia's largest small-developer community (~15,000 members). Has said publicly he **spent $120,000+ on courses/mentors himself early on** and found most "generic and overpriced" — which shaped how he built PDN. Useful as a sanity check before Brad spends money on any paid program.

## Key takeaways to fold into the main strategy
1. **Satterley's build-then-sell-then-develop sequence is the closest documented match to Brad's stated path** — worth studying in more depth than a one-paragraph summary if Brad wants a literal template.
2. **The "pair with a numbers person before hiring staff" pattern held up strongly across a second, independent round of research** — reinforces the Part 1 recommendation from the main brief to find a finance-minded partner early rather than hiring reactively.
3. **Graya's STAC Capital content series remains the single most tactically useful piece of media found across both rounds** for understanding how a small builder-developer actually structures debt.
4. **Two of the most locally relevant Hunter/Newcastle figures (Arnaout, McDonald) have essentially no public content** — their playbooks aren't accessible by listening; only by asking.
